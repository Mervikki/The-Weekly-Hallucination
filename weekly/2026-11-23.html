Microsoft · The Event

Satya Nadella opened Ignite in San Francisco on Tuesday, November 17, with something other than a product. According to GeekWire, he first addressed the Outlook outage that cut off email in more than 70 countries the week before, saying Microsoft had “work to do” on resilience. He did not explain what went wrong; that had come in a preliminary report, and a final one was due on November 20.

Then came the thesis. In his edited remarks, Nadella calls Copilot “the new OS for work”: chat, coworking, coding and an autonomous agent in one place, with all of Office built in. Microsoft’s Copilot post says Home and Office in Copilot are generally available now, Code will be in “the coming weeks,” and Autopilot, an always-on agent with its own identity and memory, is only beginning to reach Frontier programme customers.

Underneath sits a long list of plumbing: Microsoft IQ for shared business context, Agent 365 for governing agents, the generally available Perception security system, an AI Transparency Initiative with 40 organisations, and Cobalt 200 virtual machines on Microsoft’s own chips. Salesforce brought its CRM into Copilot through a plugin in public preview, with Accenture as pilot customer.

Simply stated, anything you can do in Claude or ChatGPT, you can do better with Copilot and for less. — Judson Althoff, per GeekWire, about two companies whose models Microsoft also sells

The most consequential part was commercial. Microsoft resells OpenAI and Anthropic models and owns a stake in both, and Nadella announced GPT-6.2 for Foundry from the same stage. Yet the keynote’s argument, as GeekWire reported, was that customers should do their everyday work in Copilot instead, increasingly on Microsoft’s own MAI models.

Ignite did not reveal a breakthrough model. It revealed a sales posture: Microsoft wants to be the shelf, the store brand and the checkout. That works only as long as customers believe the store is neutral about the shelf.

Pricing · The Bill

The Cost Chart Was Paid For

By The Editor, Business Desk · November 23, 2026

Copilot chief Jacob Andreou showed estimated monthly costs per business user, including subscriptions and usage fees: $30 for an everyday Copilot user against $81 for ChatGPT and $205 for Claude. GeekWire notes the figures come from research Microsoft commissioned from GigaOm on modelled workloads, using enterprise pricing rather than individual subscriptions. Microsoft is also selling the cure: Agent 365 Business Analytics and a Cost Management tool to show and cap the spend.

Models · The House Brand

MAI Grades Itself Against the Labs It Resells

By The Editor, Models Desk · November 23, 2026

Microsoft AI released seven models. It says MAI Code 2 reaches 96% of Claude Opus 5.5’s performance on its chosen coding benchmarks at 5% of the price, and that MAI Cyber 2 Flash beats GPT-6 Astra on one security benchmark at under 2% of the cost per task. The company also says Code 2 is “explicitly not yet tuned” for harder science and very long agent tasks. MAI Licensed Weights will let selected customers tune Microsoft models on their own data; no date or price was given.

The caveat is the most useful line on the page: a cheap model for routine work is a real product. A vendor-picked benchmark average is not proof that it is the same product as the one it is priced against.

What wasn't announced

  • A Maia 300 date. Microsoft unveiled the chip on October 12 with deployments from 2027; Ignite repeated its efficiency claim but added no availability date.
  • What broke Outlook. Nadella apologised but, per GeekWire, did not discuss the cause; the final report was left for November 20.
  • Prices for the agents. We found none in Microsoft’s posts for Autopilot, Workspaces or Licensed Weights; Copilot Code itself is still weeks away.
  • A dated pre-event wish list. We found no reliable pre-keynote reporting on what Ignite would ship, so this box lists gaps in what Microsoft announced, not missed rumours.

Finance · Anthropic

The IPO Range Was a Starting Bid

By The Editor, Finance Desk · November 23, 2026

Anthropic priced its shares at $83 on November 18, above the proposed $70–$78 range, and began trading on Nasdaq as ANT the next day. The stock opened at $111.50 and closed its first day at $97.17, still 17% above the offer price. The registration statement became effective on November 18; the IPO closed on November 20.

After underwriters exercised their full option, the offering covered 1.127 billion shares and produced about $93.5 billion in gross proceeds, of which Anthropic says it received about $91.6 billion. A separate $26 billion private placement to seven companies, including Google, Microsoft, Nvidia and TSMC Partners, brought the combined total to about $119.5 billion. The selling shareholders’ portion went to them, not the company.

That is a record-sized financing event and a very public test of appetite for AI infrastructure and model spending. It is not revenue, earnings or evidence that the promised returns have arrived; the first trading day also ended well below its opening print.

The prospectus supplied a price. The market supplied a second one before lunch and a third by the close.

The goal is to make the whole industry better, regardless of whether Anthropic is winning or losing at any given time. — Dario Amodei at the Nasdaq debut, beside a rather large financing round

Models · Frontier

Three Flagships Arrived; “Available” Had Three Meanings

By The Editor, Models Desk · November 23, 2026

OpenAI put GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol in ChatGPT Work and Codex for Plus, Pro, Enterprise and Edu users, and in its API on November 17. Astra’s standard API rate is $8 per million input tokens, $0.40 cached input and $40 output, a 20% cut on input and output from GPT-6 Astra; Sol keeps the earlier Sol rates of $2, $0.10 and $10. Google’s Gemini 4 Neon followed on November 18: $3 input, $0.15 cached input and $15 output per million tokens, with a one-million-token context window. It is rolling out in Google’s API and AI Studio, while app and Antigravity access is for paid Pro and Ultra subscribers.

Alibaba said Qwen4-Max was available November 16 through QwenCloud, QwenWork, QwenStudio and its API. The company describes a 2.4-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts model with 71 billion active per token and a one-million-token context; its release listed a cached-input price of $0.075 per million tokens, not a complete rate card. Smaller open-weight Qwen4 models are promised later, not shipped with this launch.

The labs publish prices beside claims about their own benchmarks. The prices are useful; the performance claims remain the sellers grading their own exams.

Nvidia · Earnings

Nvidia Had $115.8 Billion in Revenue and an Interim CEO

By The Editor, Business Desk · November 23, 2026

Nvidia reported third-quarter fiscal 2027 revenue of $115.8 billion on November 17, up 103% from a year earlier and 20% from the previous quarter. Its data-centre business supplied $107.6 billion of that total. The report was Colette Kress’s first as interim CEO after Jensen Huang’s abrupt departure the week before; Nvidia has not announced a permanent successor.

The company guided to $130 billion in revenue for a fourth quarter that includes an extra week. Nvidia said it was not assuming any data-centre compute revenue from China in that outlook. The same release says its Vera Rubin systems have begun shipping, another reminder that the company is selling not just chips but the timetable on which customers can get them.

A temporary chief executive can deliver permanent-looking numbers. It does not answer who owns the next call when the numbers stop doing the talking.

Safety · Policy

One Company Has a Stop Button; California Wants a State One

By The Editor, Safety Desk · November 23, 2026

OpenAI’s November 18 account of frontier-model risk says it trains frontier models without live internet access, monitors inference for attempts to reach the internet, and pauses a workload if monitoring detects a breach. It says a central tool, Control Room, can stop model output within 35 seconds of a halt instruction. The post also acknowledges the limit: monitoring and containment have to work together because a sandbox is not guaranteed to hold.

That same day, a California report recommended baseline safety standards, independent evaluations, verified shutdown protocols and emergency state shutdown authority; it also proposed the power to cap risky recursive self-improvement. These are recommendations, not enacted requirements. One company describes controls it says it uses; the state is asking for powers it does not yet have.

The useful question is not whether a stop button exists. It is who can press it, what evidence reaches them, and whether anyone outside the operator gets to check the answer.
